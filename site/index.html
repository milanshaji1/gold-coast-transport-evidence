Where should road-safety investigation start?

The city is split into 500 m squares. Each square is ranked by serious crashes in 2021–2023, and the ranking is then checked against 2024.

20 squares, 0.35% of the city, held 7.9% of 2024's serious crashes.

That is about 23 times the city average. Clustering caught 54 of 695 against 55 for simple counts, so the simpler method stays. Most of these squares are on state-controlled roads, mainly the Pacific Motorway.

Who manages these roads

The Department of Transport and Main Roads (TMR) manages state-controlled roads such as the Pacific Motorway. The City manages local roads. A list the City can act on needs to separate the two.

Method and data quality

Data

    The data covers police-reported casualty crashes. The snapshot has only six months of 2025, so 2025 is left out. The source is revised over time, so older years may differ from what was published back then.

    The source's Gold Coast label can disagree with the current boundary. Every serious crash the source places in the Gold Coast stays in the totals.

    An uncertainty model that didn't work

    Smoothing every square with the same prior also leaves the ranking unchanged.

    Other tools

    A small read-only MCP (Model Context Protocol) server exposes these results to AI tools. A Power BI report and BigQuery spatial queries were checked against the same data snapshot.

    Method, reproducibility and sources

    Coordinates are converted from GDA2020 to MGA zone 56 so distances are in metres. The 500 m grid has a fixed origin that doesn't depend on where crashes are. DBSCAN uses a 250 m neighbourhood and at least five crashes, chosen on 2023.

    Road names and road managers come from the same crash records: the streets most often recorded in each square, and the source's controlling-authority field.

    Without traffic volumes the counts cannot measure risk per trip. Choosing a treatment would need an engineering assessment; this study does not estimate causes or crash reductions.
